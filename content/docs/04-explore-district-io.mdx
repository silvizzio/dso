District IO puts offices, techno labs, homes, shops, a hotel and public space all on one site. That's a lot, and different visitors come looking for very different things. A company that needs lab space, a family looking for a home and a hotel operator will each care about a different corner of the complex, and none of them wants to wade through the rest to find it.

Shown as a single block, the whole thing is too big to take in. So the platform lets people explore it one **phase** at a time. A phase is a section of District IO that is designed, built and handed over as one package. It takes three taps to find the phase that matches what you're after, and you can ignore everything else.

## Why phases and not buildings or floors

District IO could have been split up by building, by use or by floor. Phases turned out to be the most useful way in.

For one thing, each phase has its own completion date. Picking a phase is really also picking when you'd move in, so the choice carries more meaning than it first appears.

Each phase also has a clear character. Some are mainly workplaces, one holds the homes, and another holds the places people will visit. A visitor can tell at a glance whether a phase is even relevant to them.

Finally, it's how the complex will be sold. The sales and leasing team talks about District IO phase by phase, so a visitor who has explored it that way turns up to the first meeting already speaking the same language.

## Finding your phase

Start by selecting District IO, either on the map or in the list of areas of interest. The map flies over to the complex and drops a marker on each phase, and the left panel turns into a list of the phases in the order they'll be delivered.

![District IO selected: a marker for each phase on the map, and the list of phases in the left panel](/dso/api/img/03-future-lod3-district-io.jpg)

It's worth reading that list from top to bottom. Each phase has a short summary and an expected completion date, and taken in order they tell the story of the complex: it starts out as a place to work and live, and gradually turns into a destination.

When something looks right, tap the phase in the list or on the map. The map moves in to show what the phase contains, and the right panel explains it.

![a phase selected: its parts on the map, and the right panel explaining what the phase is for](/dso/api/img/03-future-lod3-district-io-1a.jpg)

From here you can go three ways. You can step through the delivery years to see when the phase will be ready, drill down into its buildings, or send an enquiry about it straight away.

## What the right panel tells you about a phase

The panel answers questions in the order people tend to ask them.

First, what the phase is for. It lists what the phase contains, so you can tell straight away whether you're looking at workspace or housing.

Next, how big it is. The panel gives two areas. Gross floor area (GFA) counts all the floor space inside the buildings. Gross leasable area (GLA) counts only the space a tenant can actually rent, and that's the figure that matters when you're talking about a lease.

Last, where it stands. You'll see the expected delivery date and which design stage the phase has reached, so nobody mistakes a drawing for a finished building.

Purpose comes first on purpose. Size and status don't mean much until you know the phase is relevant to you.

## Why this helps both sides

For the visitor, a huge complex becomes something they can find their way around. They skip past the phases that don't concern them and spend their time on the one that does.

The sales team gets a useful signal too. Whichever phase a visitor opens says a lot about what they're after, often before they've spoken to anyone. If they go on to enquire, that phase is attached to the enquiry automatically.

A few details make this work smoothly. The map and the list stay in step, so people can navigate by location or by name. The list follows the delivery order, which means reading it also tells you how the complex will grow. And every phase shows its status, so it's always clear that the numbers are projections.
