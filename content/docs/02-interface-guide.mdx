Most people will come across the Digital Twin at an exhibition stand or in a sales centre. They will usually have no one to guide them and only a few minutes to spare. The platform holds an entire district's worth of buildings, data and media, and if all of that appeared at once, most visitors would walk away within seconds.

To avoid this, the interface reveals detail gradually, while keeping the same layout at every stage. A visitor learns how the screen works in the first minute, and from then on they can go as deep as they like without having to learn anything new.

## Three principles behind every screen

1. **The map is the product.** It fills the screen and always stays the centre of attention. Panels sit at the edges and never cover the area the visitor is looking at.
2. **The left side is for choosing, and the right side is for explaining.** Visitors decide what to look at using the left panel or the map, and the right panel then tells them about what they have chosen.
3. **Detail follows interest.** The first view is deliberately calm. More detail only appears when the visitor zooms in or selects something.

Each principle saves the visitor from having to work something out. The first answers "where should I look?", the second answers "where can I read about this?", and the third answers "which of all this is relevant to me?"

## Three levels of detail

The map has three levels of detail. Each one plays a specific role in the visit, and visitors move between them by zooming in or out or by selecting a property.

### Level 1 draws people in from a distance

![the first level: the whole district and a single band of headline figures](/dso/api/img/02-lod1.jpg)

The first level shows the entire district, with a single band of headline figures along the bottom. There are no panels to figure out. Each figure comes with a row of small icons, so the band is easy to read from several steps away. The goal here is simple: to get someone walking past to stop and touch the screen.

### Level 2 helps the visitor get their bearings

![the second level: the district core, with District IO at the centre](/dso/api/img/02-lod2-dso-1.jpg)

The second level brings the view closer to the heart of the district, where District IO sits among the existing buildings. Its role is orientation. Before any detail competes for attention, the visitor can see exactly where the new complex will be and what surrounds it.

### Level 3 opens the full interface

![the third level: left panel, map, map controls and right panel](/dso/api/img/04-now-lod3-overview.jpg)

The third level is where the full interface appears, and where visitors explore, compare and take action. Every chapter in the Future, Now and Past sections takes place at this level.

## What each part of the screen does

| Region | What it does | Why it works this way |
|---|---|---|
| Left panel | Controls what the map shows | Keeps all choices in one place |
| Map | Shows the district and whatever is selected | Keeps the visitor oriented at all times |
| Right panel | Describes the selection and holds the actions | Keeps reading and acting together |
| Map controls | Change the view: zoom, basemap, timeline and street view | Keep view settings separate from content |
| Header | Shows the date, time and current conditions in the district | Makes it clear the map shows the present, not a recording |

The left panel also gives visitors a way back. When someone opens a property, the panel changes to a list of what is inside it, with a Back link at the top. However deep a visitor goes, the way out is always visible, so they never feel stuck.

The map and the left panel always stay in sync. Selecting a property on the map highlights it in the list, and selecting it from the list moves the map to it. This means people who think in terms of places and people who prefer lists both get to the same result.

## The right panel always follows the same order

1. **Status and name**, so the visitor knows what they have selected and whether it is available.
2. **Images**, so they can see it before reading about it.
3. **A short description** of what it is and what it is for.
4. **Key figures**, for visitors who want a sense of scale.
5. **Media**, including videos and, for buildings that already exist, live cameras.
6. **Actions**, such as Enquire and, where available, building data and the interior tour.

Because the order never changes, reading the panel quickly becomes second nature. Once a visitor has read one panel, they know where to look on the next, and it takes them less and less time to understand each new property.

## Status labels show what a visitor can do next

Every property and every space has a **status label**: a single word that tells the visitor whether it exists and whether it can be taken.

| Label | Meaning | What the visitor can do |
|---|---|---|
| Available | Space that can be leased now | View it, take the interior tour and enquire |
| Under offer | An offer is currently in progress | Enquire in case the offer falls through |
| Leased | No space is currently free | Enquire about future availability |
| Coming | Not yet built, and construction has not started | Explore it and make an off-plan enquiry |
| Completed | Shown in Past, with its year of completion | See it as part of DSO's delivery record |

The label answers the visitor's first question before they even ask it: can I have this? It also determines what they can do next, so the interface never offers an action that does not apply.

## The Enquire action is always in the same place

Enquire sits at the bottom of every right panel, and it stays on screen during street view and interior tours as well. It is the only action available in every timeline state and at every level of detail.

The reason is commercial. The platform loses much of its value if a visitor has to hunt for a way to act at the very moment they make up their mind. Keeping Enquire in a fixed position means people find it without having to think.
