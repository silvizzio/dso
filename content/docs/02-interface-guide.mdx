Most people will meet the Digital Twin at an exhibition stand or in a sales centre. Nobody will be there to explain it, and they'll give it a minute or two at most before deciding whether it's worth their time. Meanwhile the platform holds an entire district's worth of buildings, figures, photos and video. Put all of that on screen at once and people will walk straight past.

So the interface holds most of it back. Detail appears in stages as the visitor shows interest, but the layout of the screen never changes. Someone who figures out the screen in their first minute can keep going deeper without having to learn anything new.

## The ideas behind the layout

The map takes up the whole screen and stays the centre of attention the entire time. Panels sit along the edges and never cover the spot the visitor is looking at.

The left side is where you choose, and the right side is where you read. Visitors pick what to look at from the left panel or by tapping the map, and the right panel then tells them about it. After a couple of taps most people stop thinking about where to look.

The first view is kept deliberately quiet. Extra detail only appears when someone zooms in or taps on something, so a first-time visitor isn't asked to sort through information they didn't ask for.

## The three zoom levels

The map has three levels of detail, and each one does a different job during a visit. Visitors move between them by zooming or by tapping on a property.

### Level 1: the whole district

![the first level: the whole district and a single band of headline figures](/dso/api/img/02-lod1.jpg)

This is the view people see from across the room. It shows the whole district with a single strip of headline figures along the bottom, and there are no panels to figure out. Each figure has a small row of icons next to it so it can be read from a few steps back. All this level really has to do is get someone to stop walking and touch the screen.

### Level 2: getting your bearings

![the second level: the district core, with District IO at the centre](/dso/api/img/02-lod2-dso-1.jpg)

The second level moves in towards the centre of the district, where District IO will sit among the buildings that are already there. The point here is orientation. Before any panels or figures appear, the visitor gets a clear picture of where the new complex is and what's around it.

### Level 3: the full interface

![the third level: left panel, map, map controls and right panel](/dso/api/img/04-now-lod3-overview.jpg)

At the third level the full interface opens up. This is where people explore, compare and eventually get in touch, and it's where every chapter in the Future, Now and Past sections takes place.

## What each part of the screen is for

| Region | What it does | Why it's set up this way |
|---|---|---|
| Left panel | Controls what the map shows | All the choices live in one place |
| Map | Shows the district and whatever is selected | The visitor always knows where they are |
| Right panel | Describes the selection and holds the actions | Reading and acting happen in the same spot |
| Map controls | Zoom, basemap, timeline and street view | Changing the view is kept apart from the content |
| Header | Date, time and current conditions in the district | Makes it obvious the map is live, not a recording |

The left panel also gives people a way back. When you open a property, the panel turns into a list of what's inside it, with a Back link at the top. However deep someone goes, the way out stays visible, so nobody ends up stuck on a screen they don't know how to leave.

The map and the left panel are always linked. Tap a property on the map and it lights up in the list. Tap it in the list and the map flies to it. Some people naturally think in places and others prefer to scan a list, and both get to the same result.

## The right panel reads the same way every time

The right panel always follows one order. It starts with the status and name, so you know what you've picked and whether it's available. Then come images, because people like to see a place before reading about it. After that there's a short description, then the key figures for anyone who wants a sense of scale. Media comes next, which means videos and, for buildings that already exist, live cameras. The actions sit at the bottom: Enquire, and where they apply, building data and the interior tour.

Keeping that order fixed pays off quickly. By the second or third property, visitors know exactly where to look for what they want, and each new panel takes them less time to read than the last.

## Status labels

Every property and every space carries a one-word status label. It tells the visitor whether the place exists yet and whether they can have it.

| Label | Meaning | What the visitor can do |
|---|---|---|
| Available | Space that can be leased now | Look around it, take the interior tour and enquire |
| Under offer | Someone has made an offer that hasn't completed yet | Enquire in case the offer falls through |
| Leased | Nothing is free at the moment | Enquire about future availability |
| Coming | Not built, and construction hasn't started | Explore it and make an off-plan enquiry |
| Completed | Shown in Past, with its completion year | See it as part of DSO's delivery record |

The label answers the first thing on anyone's mind, which is "can I actually get this?", before they have to ask. It also decides which actions show up, so the screen never offers something that doesn't apply.

## The Enquire button stays put

Enquire sits at the bottom of every right panel, and it stays on screen during street view and the interior tours too. It's the only action you'll find in every timeline state and at every zoom level.

The reasoning is simple. The whole point of the platform is lost if someone decides they're interested and then can't find how to say so. Keeping the button in one fixed place means people reach for it without thinking.
