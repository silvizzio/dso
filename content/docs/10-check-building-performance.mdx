Buildings are usually marketed on how they look. But once you're a tenant, how a building runs matters just as much. It decides whether the office is comfortable in August, whether things break down, and how big the bills are. That information normally stays with the facilities team, and prospective tenants rarely get to see it.

The platform shows it to them. Building Info pulls live data from the building management system (BMS), which is the software that controls a building's cooling, lighting and power. It turns that stream of readings into a handful of simple signals anyone can make sense of in a few seconds.

![live building data: overall health at the top, and each floor compared below](/dso/api/img/04-now-lod3-available-technohub-4-building-level.jpg)

## Why live data carries more weight

A brochure can describe a building as comfortable and efficient. Live data shows whether that's true today. Visitors can see whether the building's systems are being monitored, whether the spaces are at a comfortable temperature, and how much energy it's using.

It's also hard to dress up. A monthly report can be tidied before anyone outside sees it. A live reading can't, and people trust it more for exactly that reason.

## How to read it

Open Building Info from any available property. The health signals at the top give you the overall picture: whether the systems are being monitored, whether the spaces are comfortable, and how much power the building is drawing at that moment.

Below that are charts comparing the floors. Each chart shows one measure for every floor, from the ground floor up, so you can scan down and look for the floor that doesn't match the others. A floor running warmer than the rest might prompt a question about the cooling. One using noticeably more energy might prompt a question about what's driving it. And a floor with fewer people on it might simply be where the free space is.

## Comparing floors, not chasing targets

The charts compare floors with each other, not with an industry benchmark. That way nobody needs to know what a "good" reading looks like. Anyone can spot the floor that stands out, and it gives them a sensible question to put to the commercial team.

The same thinking runs through the whole view. Visitors aren't facilities managers, so instead of a full dashboard with every reading, they get a few plain signals they can understand.

## What it says about DSO

For a tenant, it means seeing how well a building is run before they sign anything. A comfortable, well-monitored building is a nicer place to work and is usually cheaper to run too.

For DSO, opening up this data shows confidence in how its buildings are managed. Very few landlords let prospective tenants see their building data, so it sets DSO apart in a crowded market.
